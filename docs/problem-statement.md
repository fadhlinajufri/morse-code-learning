## Problem Statement
Individual with severe motor disabilities such as Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) often cannot write or use their arm which limits them to communicating the usual way (Pavithraa, & Kujani, T., 2026). Eye blinks can help because blinking often remains controllable even in the later stages of motor impairment. Nonetheless, it only works if the person has already learned the code while beginners will find it hard because every letter and number is a different dot-dash pattern that must be memorised and is easily confused with others (Umale, G., Kulkarni, P., Tyagi, A., Korgaonkar, A., & Tikale, D, 2026). As a result, a person who can still blink may be unable to communicate simply because they have not learned the code. This matters because losing the ability to communicate makes these users dependent on others to understand their needs.

## Users and Scope
Main users and stakeholders
•	Learners with severe motor impairment : Primary users who learn Morse code through blinks
•	Caregivers and family members : Help set up the system and support practice 
•	Supervisor : Assess the system’s functionality and accuracy 
•	Developer : Builds, tests and maintains the system

# In scope
•	Detecting short and long blinks with a standard webcam, facial landmarks and the Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR)
•	Lessons that introduce a few randomly selected letters (A–Z) or numbers (0–9)
•	Blink-based exercises with immediate feedback
•	Evaluation of blink detection accuracy, exercise accuracy, learning improvement and usability with selected users.

# Out of scope
•	Full text entry or general communication features
•	Specialised hardware (eye trackers, EEG, wearable sensors)
•	Symbols, punctuation and prosigns beyond A–Z and 0–9
•	Clinical trials or medical-grade validation
